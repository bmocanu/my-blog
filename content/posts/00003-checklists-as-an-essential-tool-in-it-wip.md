On October 30th, 1935, a Boeing aircraft named Model 299 (later called [B-17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_B-17_Flying_Fortress) or, more romantically, the Flying Fortress) of the Army Air Corps, took off, climbed and climbed until it stalled, then nosed over and crashed, killing the two pilots and injuring the other observers aboard. The cause? A simple and basic mistake, which was not supposed to happen, considering the skills of the two pilots and the many-months experience they had with the Model 299.

## Boeing Model 299 (B-17)

That crash marks the point when a very simple yet effective technique is introduced in the American aviation: the use of technical pre-flight checklists for pilots. You see, the reason for that error (and of many other crashes before that one) was because the pilots were too skilled, too sure on their capacity of handling a complex machinery that the Model 299 was. Too skilled to check the basic stuff and to avoid a catastrophy from happening.

The usage of pre-flight checklists had a major success in aviation. This led to implementing the technique in many other industries, with the medical one being the most prominent.

Analysing the industries where the checklist usage was effective allows for a profile to emerge: very skilled people work there, in complex activities, part creative, part very technical, part based on knowledge of how complex systems are built and how they operate. These people sometimes forget even the basic things and mistakes happen, some of them very expensive and very hard to correct.

It looks like the IT industry fits that profile just fine, right? I will get to that in a minute.

To learn more about the use of checklists in aviation and other industries, check the [Hidden Brain podcast, episode Check Yourself](https://www.npr.org/player/embed/560014313/560853679), on October 30th, 2017 (hey… what do you know?…same date 🙂).

Now, besides the characteristics of the industry where checklists are best applied, there are also the characteristics of the actual checklists. Many factors can influence the success of a technique like this one. Here is a summary:

- the checklist needs to be technically appropriate, easily understood by the people who will use it (in most cases it is very technical, filled with acronyms and jargon terms), with items short enough to be easy to read and grasp, without leaving room for confusion or generality.
- the checklist needs to be willingfully accepted by the people. This means imposing the checklist by rule will not work.
- the checklist application needs to be properly validated.
- the checklist needs to be fitted properly into the flow of the people, cannot be an element of disturbance. If this happens, then the checklist will soon be stopped being check and get useless

The IT industry is no stranger to the technique of checklists. More to follow…
